Creative writer · plot grid · four artboards · obsidian default light theme

A scene is a row. A thread is a column. An empty run down a column is a subplot the reader has been allowed to forget.

The timeline answered one question — who is on stage — with a matrix that had no room to say anything else. The plot grid keeps that matrix, folds it into a single column, and gives the freed width to the threads themselves: what each one is doing in each scene, even when it is off the page. Same shell as the other five panels: scope, state line, tools, sibling jumps, docked side column.

  1. 01
    Time and POV moved left, and say where they come from
    Both sit in the derived block with a mono subtitle naming their front-matter key — "from: when", "from: pov". Nothing is guessed from a column's name, and a thread called Time is now just a thread. The keys are bound in the project note, and the column menu's "Use as POV" is how a thread column gets promoted deliberately.
  2. 02
    Selection is a row, because the properties are the scene's
    Clicking a scene selects the whole row — tinted faintly, ringed on the sticky columns — and the cell section becomes a scene inspector: time, POV, and the stops this scene makes in every thread. A cell selection still shows role, anchor and note, as in artboard 1.
  3. 03
    POV is a 3px chip, not a wash
    The chip rides the left edge of the sticky scene column, so POV travels with the row's subject and survives horizontal scrolling. Row tint is gone, which returns the cell background to a single job: selection and proposal state.
  4. 04
    Reversal is a downward triangle
    ▶ plant points forward at a promise, ◀ payoff points back at one, ▼ reversal turns the promise over — same triangle family, same accent, three unambiguous directions. Shown on "Ilse arrives; the quarrel" and stated in the key.
  5. 05
    The sparkle lives in the column it fills
    Hovering a thread header reveals a sparkle and a chevron — fill this one, or open its menu. No "which column?" step. The head-row Fill… keeps its multi-column job and now says "all columns" beneath it so the two are never confused.
  6. 06
    Plot is pinned beside scene
    Two frozen columns mean the row keeps both its name and its event no matter how far right the threads run, and the pin glyph plus "pinned" label in the header says the state is the writer's choice rather than a fixed layout. The panel lists which columns are pinned.
  7. 07
    The column menu teaches its own commands
    Eight rows, each with its command name in mono underneath, exactly as the ⋯ menus in the review. Accept all carries the proposal count; delete is the only destructive-coloured item. Every one is bindable by the writer, and none arrives bound.
  8. 08
    Still scrolling, on purpose
    Nine columns exceed the pane at 1440, which is the case for pinning rather than against it. The cast column stays folded at the far right where it costs nothing until asked for.

  1. 01
    Kinds are groups, not badges
    Each kind's first column carries the group eyebrow and its fold chevron, with a 1px group rule down its left edge; the fold pills also sit in the eyebrow row with their keys, so a e t s are visible before they are needed. Free is shown at zero rather than hidden, following the review's rule that absences are stated once and quietly.
  2. 02
    The main theme reads as an eyebrow
    "What we owe the dead" sits above the grid with its scene count and the pin glyph repeated in its column header, so the book's argument is the first line of the view and still findable as a column.
  3. 03
    Arc columns borrow the character's dot, not their colour
    The dot in the header binds the column to the cast member; the strip in the sticky column still reports POV. Two dots of the same blue in different places, each with one job.
  4. 04
    Present, unmoved is a sentence, not a symbol
    Marta at Dinner and Anna at The quarry are on the page with nothing moving in their arc. The mark is a hollow dot plus two faint words — legible without the key, and quiet enough to ignore in a scene where standing still is the point. It is a toggle, because on a first draft it would otherwise fire constantly.
  5. 05
    A reading is an offer, and it expires by being typed over
    Unopened, it is a dashed box with a note glyph saying a reading waits. Opened, the model's sentence is the placeholder — greyed, italic, unselectable — and the writer's first keystroke replaces it. There is no accept, so no route by which the model's prose becomes the book's prose.
  6. 06
    The reading shows its evidence and where it lives
    Quote, paragraph number, model, date, and "Story map.md" in the corner. Open scene at the quote and dismiss are the only two actions, both keyed, and the model line in the panel states plainly that it never writes to a chapter.
  7. 07
    Three diamonds for the anchor, three triangles for the role
    ◇ plan, ◆ verified, ◈ broken — one shape family, filled as certainty increases, and never the same shape as a role. Broken adds one red line of explanation in the cell, because a writer who sees the mark will want to know whether they cut the line or reworded it.
  8. 08
    Audit view is a lens, not a screen
    Same rows, same columns, same scroll; only the leading marks and the header counts change, and the toolbar button stays lit with its key beside it. Headers read "5 of 8 · 3 verified · 1 broken", so a column's honesty is legible from the header alone.
  9. 09
    The key list is a panel, and it names its own escape
    Bottom right, same shell as the writer board's, fourteen rows, closing line stating that tab is untouched and nothing is bound by default. It is the only place in the plugin where the whole keyboard model is visible at once.

Where I disagree, again

  • Roles cannot do the arc's work as literally as the brief asks. Want, lie, turn and truth are four ideas mapped onto three marks, and a lie is not a want — it is the opposite. In the key I named them want or lie, turn, truth, which is honest about the collision but still asks one glyph to carry two opposed meanings. If arcs matter this much, give arc columns their own four-role set and let subplots keep plant, reversal, payoff.
  • Kind should be a property, not a heading prefix. "## Arc: Anna" makes the kind part of the name, so renaming the character renames the column and retyping the prefix silently re-kinds it. A front-matter list, or a kind field per thread, keeps Story threads.md readable and stops a typo from reclassifying an arc as a free thread. I drew the grid as specified, but the panel says "Story threads.md" rather than showing the prefix as the source of truth.
  • Present, unmoved will cry wolf on a first draft. On the sample it fires twice in eight scenes; on a real manuscript with six arcs it will fire on most cells, because most scenes do not move most arcs. It earns its place as a revision tool, so it is a toggle here — and it should probably default off until a column has at least one verified stop.
  • Broken deserves a repair path, not just a colour. "Anchor no longer in the prose" tells the writer what happened but not what to do. The one key worth adding is a re-anchor flow: n walks to the broken stop, " opens the sentence picker already filtered to near-matches. Without that, audit view produces a list of red marks and no way through it.
  • Fourteen single-letter keys is more than this view can carry. r " n p x o c a t s v / ? plus arrows is most of a text editor's home row, and none of them can be bound by default, so the writer must learn all of them from one panel. I would ship arrows, enter, escape, ctrl+enter, n, x and ? — the walk-and-dismiss loop — and leave the folds and the role cycle to the command palette until the loop is proven.
  • Snapshot the grid should say where it wrote. A dated markdown table that is never read back is a good idea precisely because it is inert, but the ⋯ row needs to name the file it creates, or the writer will look for it in the grid and conclude the command did nothing.

What the grid does that the timeline and the threads chart cannot

  • It holds prose, so it can record the off-page. The matrix can say Ilse is absent; only a cell can say "Ilse's chair empty". The whole value of Rowling's spreadsheet is the thread that is working while nobody is looking at it, and a dot cannot carry that sentence.
  • Its gaps are legible as gaps. A run of empty cells down one column is the plugin's only direct picture of a subplot the reader has been made to forget. The arc chart shows a thread's shape but not its silence; the matrix shows presence but not a thread at all.
  • It is editable where it is read. The timeline and the threads chart are read-only pictures of the manuscript. The grid is a place to write the outline before the draft exists, which makes it the only one of the five panels usable on an empty project.
  • It puts derived and authored side by side. Words, plot and cast next to plant and payoff means pacing, POV balance and promise-keeping are one reading, not three tabs. The threads chart had to invent strips to gesture at that and still needed a legend.
  • It subsumes the timeline instead of competing with it. Expanding one column reproduces screen 06 exactly, which is the argument for deleting screen 06 as a separate tab.

Where I disagree with the brief

  • POV and time should not be thread columns. Special-casing two columns by name means a project with a thread called "Time" gets narrow monospace cells it never asked for, and renaming a column silently changes its behaviour. Both are properties of the scene, like words: put them in the derived block with their own editors, and let a thread called Time be a thread.
  • Row tinting by POV fights the kind colours. Anna, Marta and Ilse are all characters, so all three are #4a8fe2; tinting needs three hues that the kind palette does not have. In artboard 1 I borrowed the accent and the place green to show it working, and it already muddies the one rule from the review — colour means kind, not identity. A 3px POV chip in the sticky first column, or just the dot already in the POV cell, carries the same signal without repainting the row.
  • "Fill this column…" belongs in the column header, not the head row. The action is per column, so the sparkle in the head row has to ask which column afterwards. A sparkle in the hovered column header is one click and zero ambiguity; the head-row Fill… can stay as the multi-column route.
  • Three cell roles are enough. Plant, payoff and touch are what a writer actually files under; "reversal" sharing the payoff triangle means the grid displays a distinction it cannot show. Either give reversal its own mark or drop the word from the role dropdown.
  • A grid this wide needs column freezing, not just a sticky first column. At eight threads plus an expanded cast, POV scrolls out of view and the row loses its subject. Let the writer pin any of the derived columns, the way a spreadsheet does.
